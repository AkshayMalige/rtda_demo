Where this is going

Eleven steps, and they build in one direction: from a single machine instruction up to a 14-layer network. Each step exists because the previous one ran into a wall. Here is the whole argument on one line each, so you always know which wall you are standing at.

How to read this if it starts feeling abstract

Three colours are used consistently in every diagram, and they always mean the same thing: input data, weights, results and accumulators. Grey is waste or padding.

And whenever a number appears — 4, 8, 16 KB, 512 — it comes from one of two places: either the size of a piece of hardware, or a division you can do yourself. Nothing here is a tuning constant somebody guessed.

STEP 1

The hardware can do exactly one thing

Forget neural networks for a minute. The AIE-ML vector unit has one instruction that matters to us. It multiplies a small block of numbers by another small block of numbers.

For float32, the blocks are exactly this size:

input data weights result / accumulator wasted or padding

Three letters, and they never change meaning:

lettercountsin our networkvalue
Mrows of datahow many tracks at once4
Kthe shared dimensioninput features8
Ncolumns of weightsoutput features4
The one thing to hold onto

The hardware does not multiply a vector by a matrix. It multiplies a 4-row block by a matrix. Whether you have 4 rows of real data or not, it computes 4 rows.

Why 4×8×4? Why not 8×16×8, or bigger?

Because it is not a dial you can turn. It is the shape the silicon implements, and the compiler offers you a short menu of them — not a range.

This is the first question everyone asks, and the honest answer has two halves. The first half is simply: you don't choose it. The AIE-ML vector unit has a matrix-multiply instruction wired into the silicon, and that instruction has a fixed operand geometry. The library exposes the handful of shapes the hardware actually implements, per data type. You pick from that list. There is no mmul<8,16,8> to pick unless the hardware has one.

The second half is more interesting: why is the list short, and why are the entries that size? Because the three numbers are not free parameters — each one is pinned by a different physical resource.

So "why not bigger" becomes three separate questions with three answers: there are only four multiplier rows; a wider K would need more than one register load per operand; and a wider N would need more accumulator registers, which is exactly the resource the kernel is already spending to keep the pipeline full (you'll see it hold four accumulators live in Step 4).

The evidence that it really is fixed

This design is built twice — once in float32, once in bfloat16. bf16 is half the width, so the obvious guess is that it gets a bigger atom: twice as many numbers should fit in the same register.

It doesn't. Both precisions use the same (4, 8, 4). You can read that decision in aie_batch/gen_graph.py:

# On AIE-ML both use the SAME aie::mmul microtile, (4,8,4) -- see
# op_impls/families/matmul/common.py MICROTILE_OPTIONS -- so BATCH stays 8
# and the whole batching argument is unchanged by the precision switch.

The name to notice is MICROTILE_OPTIONS — a list, not a formula. And the practical consequence is the useful part: because the atom does not change with precision, everything you learn in this tutorial stays true when you switch to bf16. Only the speed changes.

Check yourself

If bf16 uses the same 4×8×4 atom as fp32 — same number of multiplies per instruction — why is the bf16 design 2.5× faster?

Answer

Because the atom is the same shape, not the same speed. The instruction issues faster for the narrower type: the measured initiation interval drops from 4161 ns per iteration (fp32) to 1033 ns (bf16), roughly 4×. Halving the input bytes also halves what has to be moved into the tile.

The design gets 2.5× rather than 4× — and finding out where the other 1.5× went is exactly what the performance scan in analysis/rtda_scan.ipynb was built for. It turns out bf16 is no longer compute-bound at all.

STEP 2

Why your first design was slow

Your GEMV design fed the network one track at a time. One track is one row. So M was effectively 1.

But the instruction still computes 4 rows. The other three get fed nothing useful, and their answers are thrown away.

Why no compiler flag fixes this

The instinct on seeing "25% utilisation" is to look for a switch. There isn't one, and it is worth understanding why, because it tells you what kind of problem this is.

The compiler's job is to schedule the instructions you asked for. You asked for a matrix × vector. It compiled a matrix × vector — correctly, efficiently, using the only matrix instruction the chip has. The waste is in the shape of the request, not in the translation of it. No amount of optimising a 1-row multiply turns it into a 4-row multiply; only feeding it four rows does that, and only you know where to find four rows.

Which is the general lesson: on this device the compiler cannot restructure your dataflow. Matching the hardware's shape is your job, and it is nearly all of the job.

That single fact is the whole 29× speedup. Everything that follows is just the bookkeeping needed to fill those rows.

STEP 3

Fill the rows: process 4 tracks together

The fix is embarrassingly simple to state. Instead of pushing track 0 through the whole network, then track 1, then track 2 — push tracks 0,1,2,3 through together.

Nothing about the maths changes. Track 0's answer does not depend on track 1. They just ride the same instruction.

Let's do one, with your real numbers

That's it. That is one cell of C, and the layer has 128 of them per track.

Now the important part: C[1][0] — track 1's output feature 0 — uses the very same weight column, just a different row of data. So does track 2, and track 3. The hardware loads that column once and uses it four times.

The weights get loaded once and used for all 4 tracks. That is the second win, and it is why bigger batches help even beyond filling the rows.

Check yourself

Your layer is 6 features wide, but K = 8. What happens to the other 2?

Answer

They are zero-padded. The weight matrix gets two rows of zeros, so those lanes contribute nothing. The kernel never knows — the padding is done once, when the weights are prepared. This is why parameters.h says IN_FEAT = 16 for a 6-feature input: padded up, and to 16 rather than 8 for a separate memory reason.

Why 8 tracks, not 4

The kernel keeps two 4-row blocks in flight at once, so the vector unit always has independent work while earlier multiplies are still finishing. That means the batch must be a multiple of 2×M = 8.

Ask for a batch of 4 and it gets padded to 8 and does identical work — which is exactly why batch 4 and batch 8 were measured to take the same time. 8 tracks cost the same as 4.

STEP 4

Real layers are bigger than the atom. Tile them.

A layer is a big rectangle. The atom is a small rectangle. Cover one with copies of the other — but the two directions you can move in mean different things, and that difference decides the whole design.

Our atom handles 4 tracks × 8 features → 4 outputs. A real layer is 128 features → 128 outputs. Those are not close, so let's be exact about how far apart they are.

How many atoms is one layer, actually?

Take the 128→128 layer. For one block of 4 tracks:

Atoms for 4 tracks32 × 16512

Sanity check: 4 tracks × 128 × 128the same number65,536

The last two rows agreeing is the point of the table. Tiling does not add work. It is the same multiplies, in a different order — an order the hardware can actually execute.

STOP. An atom is a moment in time, not a piece of hardware.

This is the single most important sentence in the document, and if the rest has felt like it doesn't add up, this is almost certainly why.

512 atoms does not mean 512 tiles. It means 512 executions of one instruction — a loop. One tile can run all 512, one after another, in about half a microsecond. A tile is a small processor; running a loop is what it does.

So the two numbers you are juggling live in different dimensions:

  • Atoms are TIME. How many times the instruction issues. Decided by the layer's shape ÷ the atom's shape.
  • Tiles are SPACE. How many processors hold a piece of the problem. Decided by one thing only — whether a tile's weight slice fits in one 16 KB bank.

They are almost independent. Splitting a layer over 8 tiles does not change how many atoms there are; it changes who runs which ones, and they run at the same time instead of one after another.

So how many tiles does the whole network use?

Now the budget question, with the real numbers from this repo. The VEK280 carries an XCVE2802, whose AIE-ML array is 304 engines — 8 rows × 38 columns. This design's 14 dense layers use 65 of them, plus 4 more for the two hand-written kernels, plus 13 memory tiles:

Every tile in the design runs 128 atoms per 4 tracks. That is not a coincidence — it falls straight out of the 16 KB rule. A tile holds a 64×64 weight slice, and a 64×64 slice is (64÷8) × (64÷4) = 8 × 16 = 128 atoms. The memory limit fixes the slice; the slice fixes the work.

Check yourself

65 tiles out of 304. Why not use all 304 and go ~4× faster again?

Answer

Because there is nothing left to split. Splitting is driven by the 16 KB weight limit, and every layer already sits exactly at 16 KB per tile — you cannot divide a layer further without tiles that hold less than they could.

You could of course run more copies of the whole network side by side on the spare tiles, and process four independent track streams at once. That is a throughput win, not a latency win, and this design did not need it: the array is already faster than the host can feed it. The performance scan shows the fp32 design spending only 3.4% of its time outside the array — there is no headroom to reclaim by adding tiles, because the array is not what you are waiting for.

Two directions, and this is the part that matters:

So a full dense layer is three nested loops: over tracks, over output features, and over input features. Only the last one accumulates.

Why this distinction is the whole design

Everything in Step 5 falls out of it. Ask what happens if you put two atoms on two different tiles:

  • Two atoms from across (different outputs) — the tiles never need to talk. Split them anywhere, on any two tiles, in any order. Free.
  • Two atoms from down (same output, different input slice) — one tile holds half an answer and the other holds the rest. Somebody has to add them, which means a wire between the tiles and a rule about who goes first.

So "how do I split a layer that doesn't fit" has two very different answers with two different costs, and picking between them is the actual engineering.

Why the sum stays exact

When you add up those partial sums, the running total is kept in a wide accumulator — more bits than the numbers going in. It is only rounded back down to float32 once, right at the end.

That is why the fp32 build matches the reference to 7.5e-07. You are not losing a little precision at every step.

STEP 5

One layer doesn't fit on one tile. Split it.

The limit is not speed. It is that the weights physically do not fit in the tile's memory — so the number of tiles is decided by division, before any performance thinking happens at all.

An AIE-ML tile is a small processor with its own private memory, and that memory is 64 KB — not 16. So why does every number in this section divide by 16 KB?

Because the 64 KB is banked: four banks of 16 KB. And the rule is not "the weights must fit in the tile", it is "one weight array must fit in one bank". That is a wall, not a guideline — it is a static_assert in the kernel, so exceeding it does not run slowly, it fails to compile:

static_assert(OUT_FEAT_SLICE * IN_FEAT_SLICE * sizeof(weight_t) <= 16384,
              "Weight size per tile must not exceed one AIE-ML memory bank (16 KiB)");

The other three banks are not spare. They hold the input block arriving, the output block leaving, the ping-pong copies that let the DMA fill one while the vector unit reads the other, and the stack. Weights get one bank; the working data gets the rest.

Now weigh a real layer against it. Weights are one number per (input, output) pair, so a 256→128 layer holds 256 × 128 = 32,768 of them, at 4 bytes each in float32:

Eight tiles, then. But which eight — and split which way? You already know there are two directions, and from Step 4 you know they cost different things. Now they get their names:

A picture for the cascade

Four people are adding up a long shopping list together. Each takes a quarter of the items. Person 1 totals theirs and passes the running total to person 2, who adds theirs on top, and so on. Person 4 has the answer.

The cascade is a dedicated wire between neighbouring tiles that carries that running total. Crucially it carries the full-precision total — nobody rounds to the nearest penny before passing it on. So splitting the work across 8 tiles gives bit-identical results to doing it on one.

Why 2 chains and not 4? Work the options, all three of them

A 128→128 layer is 64 KB of weights, so it needs 4 tiles. There are exactly three ways to arrange 4 tiles, and it is worth checking all of them rather than being told the answer — because the winner is not the one the latency rule picks.

arrangementwhat each tile holdsbytes per tilefits 16 KB?cascade depthDMA streams out of the memtile
1 × 4 — one chain, 4 deep32 inputs × 128 outputs16 KByes4 — worst1
2 × 2 — two chains, 2 deep64 inputs × 64 outputs16 KByes22
4 × 1 — four chains, no cascade128 inputs × 32 outputs16 KByes1 — best4 — too many

All three fit the memory exactly. So memory does not decide this one — it already did its job by saying "4 tiles".

On latency alone, 4 × 1 should win: no cascade at all, nothing waiting for a neighbour. And it is chosen against anyway, for a reason that has nothing to do with either arithmetic:

The real limit: a memory tile has 6 outgoing DMA channels

Every chain has to be fed. Four independent chains means four separate streams out of the shared buffer that holds the layer's input — and a memory tile has only 6 outgoing (MM2S) channels in total, shared with everything else that needs to read that buffer. Ask for 4 chains on the 128-wide layers and you run out.

It is written down in aie_batch/gen_graph.py, in as many words:

# cas_num is capped at 2 on the 128-wide layers: the default of 4
# saturates the 6 MM2S DMA channels of a memory tile.

So 2 × 2 is the compromise: the best cascade depth still reachable within the channel budget. The rule picked 4 × 1; a countable resource elsewhere overruled it.

That pattern — a clean principle, overruled by some other resource you have to go and count — is most of what designing for this device feels like. It is also why "why is it 2?" almost never has an answer in the layer's own arithmetic.

Check yourself

The 256→128 layer (L3) uses 2 × 4 = 8 tiles, so its cascade is 4 deep — the depth we just called "worst". Why is that acceptable there?

Answer

Because it has no choice. 256 × 128 × 4 B = 128 KB needs 8 tiles, and chains are capped at 2 by the same DMA limit — so 8 ÷ 2 = a cascade 4 deep. The options were 2 × 4 or nothing.

Note what that means for the whole design: the deepest cascade in the network is set by the widest layer, and the widest layer exists because the roll-concat doubles 128 features into 256. The cost of that concatenation shows up here, four tiles away from where it was introduced.

So which split do you pick? Two rules, and a real constraint

Memory tells you how many tiles. It does not tell you the shape. For that:

  • Prefer more chains, shorter cascades. A cascade of 4 means the last tile cannot finish until three others have gone before it — that latency is on every single pass. Independent chains have no such ordering.
  • But chains are capped at 2 here, and not for a tidy reason. Each chain has to be fed from the shared buffer, and a memory tile has only 6 outgoing DMA channels. Ask for 4 chains on the 128-wide layers and you run out of channels. The code says so out loud: cas_num is capped at 2 on the 128-wide layers: the default of 4 saturates the 6 MM2S DMA channels of a memory tile.

That is the shape of nearly every decision on this device: a clean rule, overruled by a countable hardware resource somewhere else.

This is where designs are actually decided

Not by performance intuition — by arithmetic. Weight bytes ÷ 16 KB = minimum tiles. In this whole design, every layer but the first sits at exactly 16 KB per tile. The tile counts were chosen to hit that wall.

One deliberate exception worth knowing about: the cascade lengths in this project are pinned by hand, not chosen by the tool. bf16 halves the weight bytes, so the resolver would happily give it shorter cascades than fp32 — and then the two builds would differ in parallelism as well as precision, and no comparison between them would mean anything. Pinning keeps precision the only variable.

STEP 6

Getting the weights in — and in the right order

Weights are pushed once, before the run starts, over RTP ports — the same mechanism you already used. They then sit on the tile forever, and every iteration is pure compute. Nothing about this part needed to change.

What did change is the order the numbers are stored in.

The numbers are the same. Only their order in memory changes — and it changes to match the order the kernel will ask for them in.

The kernel grabs weights 32 at a time — one whole 8×4 block, in one memory read. For that to be a single fast read, those 32 numbers must be next to each other in memory. But in a normal row-by-row weight matrix, an 8×4 block is scattered across 8 different rows.

Why not just let the hardware gather them?

Because a "gather" is not one operation. Reading 32 contiguous numbers is a single instruction. Reading 4 numbers from each of 8 different rows is eight separate reads plus the address arithmetic to work out where each row starts — and it happens inside the innermost loop, the one that runs 512 times per layer per 4 tracks.

So you have a choice about where to pay the cost of the mismatch: once, on the host, before the run starts — or several times per atom, forever. The rearrangement is the same work either way. Doing it once is free; doing it in the loop is the design.

This is the same trade as the bias in Step 7 and the reshape in Step 8, and it is worth naming: move work out of the inner loop and into setup wherever the shape lets you.

Why this matters more than it looks

If you pack the weights wrongly, the graph still runs, still produces plausible-looking numbers, and is wrong. There is no crash to tell you.

That is why this project checks it: the notebook re-packs the weights from source and compares against all 92 payloads actually loaded onto the card, expecting 0.000e+00. Do this for any model you map.

STEP 7

Bias and ReLU cost nothing

In your design these were separate kernels — a bias-add tile, a leaky-ReLU tile, each with buffers and connections. Here they vanish, and it's worth seeing how, because the trick generalises.

Bias: start the tally at the bias

Normally you compute y = W·x, then add b. Instead: set the running total to b before you start multiplying. Then every multiply-accumulate lands on top of it, and when you're done the bias is already in there.

C = bias;          // start the tally at b, not 0
C.mac(A, B);       // ... and just accumulate as normal

Cost: zero extra instructions. A whole tile, buffer and hop disappear.

Leaky ReLU: fold it into the write

The result has to be converted from the wide accumulator down to float32 anyway before being stored. The activation is applied in that same moment, while the numbers are already in registers — about three extra vector instructions total.

The general rule

Anything elementwise — bias, ReLU, scale, clamp — should be folded into the end of whatever produced the data. Never give it its own kernel.

A kernel costs you a tile, a buffer, a DMA transfer and a synchronisation point. An elementwise operation costs one instruction.

STEP 8

Moving data between layers, for free

Layer N writes its answers in one shape; layer N+1 wants them in another. On a CPU you would write a loop to shuffle them. Here you write a number, and the shuffle happens inside a transfer that was going to occur anyway.

Here's a real problem. A dense layer writes its results in 4×4 blocks (4 tracks × 4 outputs — that's the shape of C). But the next layer wants to read them in 8×4 blocks (8 features × 4 tracks — the shape of A).

Same numbers, different grouping. Somebody has to regroup them.

Why the regrouping is genuinely free, not just cheap

The data has to travel from the producing tile to the consuming tile regardless — they are different processors with different private memories. That journey is made by a DMA engine, and a DMA engine does not read straight through from start to finish. It walks the buffer following a pattern you give it: so many elements, then skip so far, then so many more.

So the reshape rides along inside a movement that was already happening and already paid for. Nothing reads the data an extra time; nothing writes a second copy; no tile spends a cycle on it. You are choosing the itinerary of a trip, not adding a trip.

That is why the sentence "tiling is an access pattern, not a copy" is worth memorising. A copy costs memory and cycles. A pattern costs a few numbers in a config struct.

You were already circling this idea with your 512 KB tile buffer and manual read/write access — that was the right mechanism. What the batched design adds is choosing the shapes so they match the atom, which is what makes the reshape free.

The same buffer can also be read from two different starting offsets — one reader takes features 0–63, another takes 64–127 — which feeds two cascade stages. The "splitter" kernel you'd otherwise write by hand is a single number in a config struct.

STEP 9

The two things that aren't dense layers

Almost everything is handled by the machinery above. Two things aren't, and they're the ones you'd still write by hand.

The roll-concat — and a warning about memory

Each track needs to be paired with the track before it. The kernel keeps the last track of one block in a static variable so the next block can reach it.

A static is a state machine. You now own its reset.

Statics live from when the program is loaded, not from when you press run. Two consequences bit this project hard:

  • Track 0 pairs with whatever came before it, not with track 49. So the hardware's roll is streaming, while the ONNX reference is circular. That single difference is the origin of every "why don't my numbers match" confusion here.
  • Running the graph twice without reloading starts with the previous run's leftovers. Fixed by pushing one dummy all-zero event through first and discarding it.

The averager — and a rule about rates

It sums 50 tracks and emits one result. But the graph requires every kernel wired to an output to write something every single iteration. It can't say "nothing this time."

So it writes zeros six times out of seven, and the real mean on the seventh. That's not a hack — it's how you express a duty cycle in a system with fixed data rates.

Why the graph insists on that, and where the 7 comes from

The whole array runs on a static schedule. Buffers, DMA descriptors and synchronisation are all laid out at compile time, on the promise that every kernel consumes and produces a fixed number of items per iteration. That promise is what makes the schedule computable at all — and it is what lets the design run with no runtime arbitration. A kernel that sometimes emitted nothing would break the arithmetic the scheduler was built from.

The 7 is just the event size divided by the batch: 50 tracks per event, padded to 56 slots, at 8 tracks per iteration = 7 iterations. So one event mean emerges every 7th iteration, and the other 6 have to say something. They say zero.

The reader downstream then has to know which frame is real — which is why the host scans for non-zero frames rather than assuming a position, and why the number 7 shows up as N_ITER in parameters.h.

And one thing deliberately NOT on the AIE

The final 128→27 layer runs on the host CPU. It operates on a single averaged vector — one row. That's M=1, the exact shape this whole design exists to avoid. It's only 3,456 multiplies, but measured at 15–19 µs on the array versus 4.2 µs for the entire 14-layer pipeline. On the host it's free.

Knowing when not to use the accelerator is part of the skill.

STEP 11

Mapping your own model

Someone hands you a network. Do the arithmetic before you write any code — the first three steps decide most of the design.

  1. Build a trusted reference first. A numpy or ONNX model you believe, and a fixed stimulus. Without it you cannot tell a mapping bug from a precision effect, and you will lose days to that confusion.
  2. Weight bytes ÷ 16 KB = minimum tiles, per layer. This is the number that decides your design.
  3. Choose the split. Prefer more independent chains (CAS_NUM) over longer cascades (CAS_LENGTH) when memory lets you — shorter chains, less latency.
  4. Batch = a multiple of 8. Below that you're padding and paying anyway.
  5. Make every dimension a multiple of 16 elements. Pad in the weight preparation, never in the kernel.
  6. Fuse everything elementwise into the producer.
  7. Match buffer shapes to what producer and consumer want, and let the DMA do the reshaping.
  8. Isolate anything stateful and write down its reset semantics before you build.
  9. Verify the weights actually arrived — re-pack from source, diff against what was loaded, expect bit-exact.
  10. Measure II, not wall-clock. II is the array's true throughput; wall-clock includes host and DMA costs that don't scale with the array.
The habit that matters most

When a number surprises you, measure the decomposition rather than reasoning about it. In the sister PL design in this repo, four changes were made to improve accuracy — and measuring them one at a time showed that three of the four were individually harmful. Nobody would have guessed that. Only the ablation showed it.
